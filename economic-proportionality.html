Research proposal · 14 November 2026

Economic proportionality and renovation risk

Could clearer competence assessment, scope boundaries and documented escalation improve consumer protection without imposing unnecessary costs?

Evidence status: The economic benefits, harms and carpentry-specific causal effects have not been quantified. This is a research proposal, not a regulatory impact statement or government-endorsed policy.

Public interest first, statutory vehicle last

First define and test the public-interest problem, while clarifying the proposed baseline registration and licensing arrangements. Then test whether a distinct remaining practitioner function exists, whether competence can be reliably assessed, and whether any intervention delivers proportionate benefits.

Compare genuine alternatives

  • No additional intervention beyond the baseline adopted by government
  • Voluntary guidance and clearer handoff practices
  • Advanced credential without additional statutory authority
  • Possible assessment or endorsement within an existing pathway, subject to primary-law feasibility verification
  • A distinct bounded class, only if evidence justifies it

What must be measured?

Verified renovation-specific harms and their decision-makers; time and cost of assessment, records and referrals; rework, disputes and delays; consumer understanding; and burdens on sole traders, experienced workers, builders, homeowners and regulators, including workforce exit, price pass-through, possible unregistered-work displacement, regulator enforcement costs and insurer underwriting effects.

Early escalation means stopping work when an unexpected condition falls outside the practitioner’s lawful scope or competence, documenting the issue and referring it to an appropriately authorised professional. It does not confer authority to approve structural or regulated work. Early escalation may prevent harm, but it can also cause unnecessary referrals, duplicated checks or delays. Both outcomes must be counted. General building defect or tribunal statistics cannot automatically be attributed to carpenter decisions.

Proposed next step

Use a controlled, independently evaluated pilot to test whether the process works, including stop rules, assessor consistency and comparisons with existing practice. A proposed 6–12 month follow-up could assess process and short-term rework, not long-term durability. Study design must address selection bias and define any confirmatory sample-size and power requirements before recruitment. A small pilot alone cannot establish long-term defect reductions.

Boundary: This proposal does not grant builder, engineer or licensed-trade authority. No regulatory vehicle has been selected. Government engagement is not endorsement.
