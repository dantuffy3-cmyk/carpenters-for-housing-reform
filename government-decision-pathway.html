Government decision pathway · capability-first model

Capability first. Statutory vehicle second.

The first government question is whether advanced residential renovation carpentry is a distinct capability worth formally assessing. Only after that question is answered should government determine the most proportionate implementation mechanism.

Review the four decisions View the progression evidence Read the RRC proposal
The immediate government request

Assess capability before choosing a vehicle

Formally examine the capability question, compare proportionate implementation vehicles and test the model under controlled conditions before considering broader implementation.

A decision sequence, not a demand for a predetermined statutory class

This framework separates the capability question from the final statutory vehicle. Government can investigate the evidence, test whether intervention is justified, compare proportionate implementation options, and authorise a limited pilot only if the safeguards are workable.

Certificate III remains the foundational carpentry qualification. Advanced Residential Carpentry capability is the reform architecture. Registered Residential Carpenter, DB-L Carpentry reform, an advanced trade credential and another government-selected mechanism remain open vehicle options.

Current Victorian building reform provides a relevant policy context because it is expanding practitioner registration and considering authorised scope, competence, qualifications, experience and accountability settings.

Current reform alignment: Advanced carpenters are not currently identified as a proposed new registration category. The relevance of the Government’s reform program is therefore policy architecture, not endorsement of this proposal.

Decision 1

Is advanced residential renovation carpentry a distinct capability worth formally assessing?

Does Victoria currently recognise and assess advanced residential renovation capability between Certificate III and domestic builder registration in a way that is clear, objective and consumer-facing?

Certificate III capability mapping

Test what the foundational qualification establishes, what renovation-relevant units are already present and where advanced assessment may still be needed.

DB-L and DB-U pathway analysis

Review how current builder pathways recognise carpentry capability, business responsibility and broader project authority.

Occupational progression comparison

Compare carpentry with other occupations that distinguish foundational trade qualification from higher assessed capability.

Practitioner evidence cases

Review documented renovation case evidence showing concealed conditions, progressive discovery and judgement demands in existing buildings.

Training and assessor evidence

Assess whether training, assessment and recognition settings can currently identify advanced renovation capability in a credible and consistent way.

Consumer, builder, insurer and regulator feedback

Test whether stakeholders identify a genuine gap, a narrower implementation issue or no intervention need at all.

Possible findings

Gap established

Evidence supports a material recognition or assessment gap between Certificate III and builder registration.

Gap partly established

Some issue is verified, but the gap may be narrower than first proposed or limited to specific renovation contexts.

Existing pathways adequate

Current arrangements may already provide a workable pathway once guidance, awareness or uptake barriers are addressed.

Further evidence required

Government may conclude that the case is incomplete and require better evidence before moving to intervention analysis.

Decision output: A published finding defining whether the issue is primarily a training gap, assessment gap, registration-design gap, market-recognition gap or combination.

Decision 2

Is government intervention justified?

Would addressing the gap materially improve consumer protection, workforce capability and regulatory clarity?

Consumer harm and confusion

Assess whether unclear role boundaries contribute to poor consumer understanding, scope misunderstanding or unsuitable practitioner selection.

Defect and escalation risk

Test whether hidden-condition work, progressive discovery and delayed escalation create avoidable defect or compliance risks.

Workforce progression

Examine whether a missing assessed progression tier affects skills development, recognition and retention for advanced carpenters.

Protection of builder responsibility

Confirm that any intervention preserves registered builder authority, handoff thresholds and project-accountability settings.

Insurance feasibility

Test whether any proposed model can be supported by suitable underwriting controls, exclusions and claims evidence.

Enforcement practicality

Assess whether the regulator could define scope, monitor compliance and respond to complaints without creating unmanageable ambiguity.

Regulatory cost and duplication

Compare implementation burden against the scale of the problem and avoid duplicating functions already performed by existing pathways.

Unintended consequences

Check whether intervention could create false consumer confidence, scope confusion, market distortion or blurred accountability.

Decision output: A proportionality assessment identifying whether action is justified and what level of intervention is appropriate.

Decision 3

If capability is established, which implementation vehicle is most proportionate?

If government concludes that the capability should be formally recognised, it can then compare four possible vehicles before selecting any pilot model.

Option A — Registered Residential Carpenter

A distinct bounded registration or recognition category.

Option B — DB-L Carpentry stream / endorsement / revised assessment

Use existing builder-registration architecture if government determines that this is the more proportionate mechanism.

Option C — Advanced trade credential

Independently assessed advanced capability without automatic expansion of statutory contracting authority.

Option D — Government-selected alternative

Another mechanism identified through consultation, registration reform or legislation.

Evaluation matrix

Consumer clarity

Would the model make responsibility boundaries easier for the public to understand?

Preservation of carpenter identity

Can advanced recognition occur without forcing carpenters to abandon their occupational identity?

Regulatory duplication

Does the model avoid creating unnecessary overlap with existing registration settings?

Implementation effort

What level of policy, legislative, administrative and training work would the model require?

Insurance

Can the model operate with credible coverage, underwriting conditions and claims oversight?

Enforcement

Can scope and exclusions be monitored and enforced in practice?

Builder interface

Does the model preserve clear handoff points to registered builders, engineers, surveyors and licensed trades?

Scope control

Can the model maintain narrow, published boundaries without drift into broader builder authority?

Assessment credibility

Would the model rely on independently verified competence rather than experience alone?

Decision output: Selection of the preferred vehicle for further testing, not a predetermined permanent implementation.

Decision 4

Should government authorise a controlled pilot?

A pilot should proceed only if the design, insurance, enforcement and accountability safeguards are workable and the pilot can test capability without granting new authority.

Pilot design principles

  • small participant cohort
  • qualified carpenters only
  • participants remain within existing lawful authority
  • verified renovation experience
  • independent assessment
  • narrow published scope
  • mandatory exclusions
  • builder and engineer handoff triggers
  • insurance confirmation
  • evidence and documentation requirements
  • consumer role-comprehension testing
  • persistent work-and-evidence record
  • proportional escalation assessment
  • audit and complaints oversight
  • defined trial period
  • independent evaluation

Success measures

  • consumer understanding
  • scope compliance
  • consumer role comprehension
  • escalation behaviour
  • defect and complaint outcomes
  • documentation quality
  • builder and trade feedback
  • insurer acceptance
  • regulator workload
  • participant competence
  • unintended consequences

Stop conditions

  • unacceptable consumer risk
  • scope creep
  • inability to secure suitable insurance
  • unclear accountability
  • poor audit compliance
  • duplication with existing registration
  • inadequate regulator capacity

Decision output: Approve, revise, defer or reject a limited pilot.

Industry and consumer stakeholders

  • scope testing
  • workforce impacts
  • consumer clarity
  • builder interfaces
  • implementation risks

What government is not being asked to approve yet

unrestricted builder authority

new-home construction by RRCs

removal of registered builders from renovation projects

waiver of permits, engineering, insurance or licensed-trade requirements

recognition based solely on years served

permanent statewide implementation before evaluation

Recommended immediate government action

Establish a short, formal scoping process to determine whether advanced residential renovation carpentry should be assessed as a defined capability and, if so, which implementation vehicle is most proportionate for further testing.

  1. Nominate a lead agency
  2. Verify the evidence base
  3. Consult targeted stakeholders
  4. Compare regulatory options
  5. Publish a pilot feasibility finding
  6. Proceed only if safeguards are workable
