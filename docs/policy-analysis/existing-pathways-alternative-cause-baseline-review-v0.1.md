## Part 1 — Purpose and Decision Use

### 1.1 Review Purpose

This review tests whether the apparent Advanced Residential Carpentry (ARC) problem:

1. is already substantially addressed by existing Victorian pathways or market mechanisms; or
2. may be primarily attributable to causes other than carpenter capability.

### 1.2 Three Tests

**A. Existing-Pathway Adequacy**

Could current statutory, educational, employment or commercial mechanisms already address the proposed capability problem?

This test requires the review to examine each available pathway individually and to determine whether the capability gap, if any, is real or is a function of insufficient evidence about existing mechanisms.

**B. Alternative Causation**

Could renovation failures or poor outcomes attributed to carpenter capability actually arise principally from another actor, system or failure mode?

Residential renovation involves multiple actors: builders, engineers, building surveyors, licensed trades, designers, owners and subcontractors. An outcome attributed to carpentry may be principally caused elsewhere.

**C. Attribution**

When can an observed problem legitimately be treated as evidence relevant to carpenter capability?

Attribution is not automatic. This review defines a governed attribution test and applies it to the available practitioner evidence cases.

### 1.3 Legitimate Null Results

This review must be capable of producing a null result. The following are legitimate outcomes:

> `EXISTING_PATHWAYS_APPEAR_SUFFICIENT`

> `INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE_TO_ESTABLISH_A_DISTINCT_GAP`

These outcomes are not failures. They are correct findings if the evidence supports them.

### 1.4 Decision Use

This review is intended to:

- support the Government Engagement Readiness File (Part 4 of v0.2);
- provide baseline evidence for any future Engage Victoria consultation response; and
- establish a governed starting position for further evidence work.

## Part 4 — Correct Legal and Regulatory Distinctions

### 4.1 Key Distinctions

The following are distinct concepts that must not be conflated:

| Concept | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Qualification** | A nationally recognised award (e.g., CPC30220) confirming competency in specified units at a standard set by the training package. |
| **Competence classification** | An assessment or recognition outcome that categorises a practitioner's capability level. Not the same as a qualification or a registration. |
| **Registration** | Statutory authority granted by the BPC under the Building Act 1993 to carry out, arrange or manage specified building work. Registration requires meeting prescribed eligibility requirements. |
| **Contracting authority** | The authority a registered person holds to enter contracts for specified building work. Derived from registration class, not from monetary threshold. |
| **Builder responsibility** | The obligations of a registered builder under the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995 and other legislation for contracted domestic building work. |
| **Engineering authority** | The authority and responsibility of a registered engineer for engineering design, advice and certification. Retained by engineers regardless of any carpentry recognition mechanism. |
| **Consumer-recognisable advanced capability** | The ability of a consumer to identify, understand and rely on a practitioner's advanced capability signal when engaging them. Not automatically conferred by qualification or registration without an accessible and understood credential. |

### 4.2 MDBC Threshold Control

The Major Domestic Building Contract (MDBC) monetary threshold defines when a domestic building contract must be in writing and comply with the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995. It is a **contract requirement threshold**, not a **practitioner authority threshold**.

> This review does not infer any practitioner registration threshold, capability level, or authority from the MDBC monetary threshold.

Confusing MDBC thresholds with practitioner capability or registration scope is an error. It is not made here.

## Part 6 — Market-Mechanism Test

### 6.1 Market Mechanisms Under Test

The following market mechanisms are tested:

- references and reputation;
- work history and portfolio;
- repeat subcontracting relationships;
- employer selection and progression;
- remuneration as a capability signal;
- procurement and prequalification;
- insurance as a capability signal;
- professional networks.

### 6.2 Observed vs Proven

| Classification | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED** | There is evidence that the mechanism exists and operates in the market. |
| **MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE** | There is evidence that the mechanism reliably identifies and signals advanced renovation capability to the relevant audience. |

These are not the same finding. Observing a mechanism does not establish that it works effectively.

### 6.3 Mechanism Analysis

**References and reputation**

MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED — carpenters and builders commonly rely on references and reputation signals in subcontracting relationships.

MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE — not established. The effectiveness of references as an advanced capability signal depends on the assessor's own capability to judge the reference. References are primarily effective within existing professional networks. They are not effective for new employers or one-off consumers without existing contact networks.

Effective for: builders and employers with existing contact networks.
Not established as effective for: one-off consumers; new employers; insurers; government procurement outside existing relationships.

---

**Work history and portfolio**

MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED — experienced builders and employers assess work history and project portfolios.

MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE — not established as a systematic signal. Portfolios are not independently verified. Assessment depends on evaluator capability. Not accessible to consumers.

Effective for: skilled builders and employers. Not established for: consumers; insurers; government procurement; new market entrants.

---

**Repeat subcontracting relationships**

MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED — repeat engagement is common in residential renovation construction.

MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE — not established as a systematic capability signal. Repeat engagement reflects commercial relationships and reliability, but does not necessarily establish advanced capability assessment. A carpenter may be repeatedly engaged for reasons other than advanced renovations capability.

---

**Employer selection and progression**

MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED — employers informally assess and develop capability over time.

MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE — not established. No publicly available evidence of systematic employer assessment practices calibrated to advanced renovation capability in Victoria.

---

**Remuneration**

MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED — higher-skilled carpenters typically command higher remuneration in the market.

MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE — not established as a reliable advanced-capability signal. Remuneration reflects demand, scarcity, negotiation and relationship as well as capability. Not accessible as a consumer information signal.

---

**Procurement and prequalification**

MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED — government and large-client procurement includes qualification requirements.

MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE — not established for advanced residential renovation carpentry specifically. Applicable to large procurement contexts; not relevant to typical residential consumer engagement.

---

**Insurance**

MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED — public liability and other insurance are required for carpentry work. From 1 July 2026, Home Warranty applies to eligible domestic building work.

MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE — not established as an advanced capability signal. Insurance requirements reflect financial risk management, not practitioner capability level. Insurers may not assess advanced renovation-specific capability in current practice.

---

**Professional networks**

MARKET_MECHANISM_OBSERVED — informal professional networks operate among experienced renovation practitioners in Victoria.

MARKET_MECHANISM_PROVEN_EFFECTIVE — not established. Network-based signals are not accessible to consumers outside those networks.

---

### 6.4 Market Mechanism Final Classification

After reviewing available evidence, the appropriate classification is:

> **MARKET_MECHANISM_PARTIALLY_SUFFICIENT** for industry-to-industry and builder-to-subcontractor capability signalling within existing professional networks.

> **INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE** to establish that market mechanisms provide an adequate capability signal to:
> - one-off consumers;
> - new employers or builders without existing relationships;
> - insurers assessing individual practitioners;
> - government procurement processes at the residential renovation level.

> **INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE** to claim a formal consumer information-asymmetry problem without further verified evidence. This is plausible but not yet proven.

## Part 8 — Carpenter Capability Attribution Test

### 8.1 Attribution Test Criteria

An incident should not be treated as evidence of a carpenter capability problem unless the available evidence supports, at minimum, all of the following:

**A.** The relevant condition or action occurred within or materially interacted with carpentry work.

**B.** The practitioner had a reasonable opportunity to observe, recognise or respond to it.

**C.** The expected response fell within carpenter competence, or within a responsibility to stop, refer or escalate.

**D.** The outcome was not wholly attributable to another practitioner or system.

**E.** Stronger carpenter capability could plausibly have altered the decision pathway or consequence.

**F.** Causation uncertainty is explicitly retained.

### 8.2 Attribution Classification

| Classification | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **DIRECT_CARPENTER_CAPABILITY_RELEVANCE** | Evidence clearly satisfies criteria A–E; causation uncertainty retained per F. |
| **MIXED_CAUSATION** | Evidence supports carpenter capability relevance alongside other causal factors; neither predominates. |
| **BOUNDARY_RECOGNITION_RELEVANCE** | The primary evidence relates to the carpenter's responsibility to stop, refer or escalate, rather than to primary workmanship or diagnostic capability. |
| **WEAK_CARPENTER_CAPABILITY_RELEVANCE** | Some criterion is not met; the connection to carpenter capability is speculative. |
| **INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE** | Insufficient information to classify. |

## Part 10 — Residual-Problem Test

### 10.1 After Accounting for Existing Pathways

After reviewing the thirteen existing pathways examined in Part 3, the following positions are supported:

**Verified (from primary sources):**
- Victoria has a statutory builder-registration pathway (DB-L Carpentry) that confers domestic-building authority and applies qualification and experience thresholds.
- A national trade qualification (CPC30220) is the standard entry credential for the trade.
- Neither pathway has been shown by public evidence to include explicit domain-by-domain assessment of advanced renovation-specific capability at the level described by the seven-domain model.

**Plausible (by inference — not proven):**
- The current public evidence base does not confirm that advanced renovation-specific diagnostic capability, professional judgement and boundary recognition are assessed in any pathway to an independently verified standard.
- A capable carpenter operating below DB-L registration may not have access to a recognisable advanced-capability signal that is consumer-visible, independently verified, or accessible outside existing professional networks.

**Not yet proven:**
- That the absence of a clearly described renovation-specific assessment standard results in worse consumer outcomes in practice.
- That one-off residential renovation consumers experience material harm attributable to the absence of an advanced carpentry credential.
- That current market mechanisms (reputation, references, employer selection) are insufficient to identify and deploy advanced renovation capability at the level required.

**Unknown:**
- The degree to which DB-L Carpentry, in actual practice, assesses diagnostic capability, professional judgement and boundary recognition (Governing DB-L Finding D preserved).
- The frequency and severity of renovation outcomes in Victoria that are attributable, under the attribution test, to carpenter diagnostic or judgement failure rather than to alternative causes.

### 10.2 After Accounting for Alternative Causation

The alternative-cause map in Part 7 identifies eighteen plausible causal pathways to renovation failure. In seventeen of eighteen causes, a competent carpenter's primary role is boundary recognition, stop/escalation, and documentation — not the prevention of the primary cause.

**Causes 16, 17 and 18** (carpenter workmanship; carpenter diagnostic failure; carpenter boundary-recognition failure) are the most directly relevant to the ARC capability question.

**Causes 1–15** are primarily attributable to other actors or system failures. For these causes, the question is not whether the carpenter caused the problem, but whether the carpenter could have detected and escalated it before consequences worsened.

### 10.3 Residual Problem Statement

The following residual problem is assessed as **plausible but not yet proven** after accounting for existing pathways, market mechanisms, alternative causation, and attribution:

> There may be a class of residential renovation decision points — particularly involving diagnostic assessment of existing conditions, professional judgement under uncertainty, and escalation of concealed conditions — where:
>
> (a) no publicly verified pathway currently provides independent confirmation that an individual carpenter has demonstrated this capability;
>
> (b) the consumer has no accessible signal to distinguish a capable from a less capable practitioner; and
>
> (c) the builder, if engaged, may not have independently assessed the carpenter's renovation-specific diagnostic and judgement capability.

This statement is classified **PLAUSIBLE** based on:
- the absence of publicly evidenced domain-specific assessment across examined pathways;
- the partial sufficiency of market mechanisms for industry-to-industry contexts and their weaker performance for consumer-facing contexts; and
- the concentration of practitioner-case evidence on boundary recognition and escalation functions.

This statement is **NOT_YET_PROVEN** because:
- the current practitioner cases are placeholders and do not provide verified statewide evidence;
- DB-L Carpentry assessment content is not sufficiently evidenced in public material to exclude the possibility that it addresses these domains in practice;
- no verified consumer-outcome data has been reviewed; and
- alternative causes have not been ruled out for any specific incident.

> The review does not manufacture a residual problem to preserve ARC. The status quo remains a legitimate outcome if evidence does not advance beyond this position.

## Part 11 — Minimum Effective Intervention Implications

### 11.1 Preamble

The following analysis does not select a preferred option. It examines what the current evidence would imply for each option, without predetermining the choice.

### 11.2 Problem-to-Intervention Mapping

If the residual issue is primarily:

**TRAINING** — educational mechanisms are the proportionate response. ARC-specific recognition may not be required.

**ASSESSMENT** — assessment mechanisms (whether within DB-L, as a separate assessment, or through a non-statutory credential) are more proportionate than new statutory classes.

**MARKET SIGNALLING** — a non-statutory recognition mechanism may be sufficient. A new statutory class may not be proportionate.

**SCOPE/AUTHORITY** — statutory recognition mechanisms are the proportionate response. A non-statutory credential would be insufficient.

**BUILDER MANAGEMENT** — ARC may be the wrong intervention. Builder supervision, selection and accountability improvements may be more direct.

**BOUNDARY RECOGNITION** — determination is needed as to whether improved assessment, clearer guidance, training, or statutory recognition is proportionate and necessary for this specific function.

### 11.3 Option Analysis

**Option A — Distinct RRC class or category**

The current evidence does not yet establish that the residual problem requires a new statutory class as the proportionate response. The problem may be one of assessment, signalling or training rather than scope or authority. A new statutory class would be proportionate if the residual issue extends to scope and authority, and if DB-L reform is assessed as insufficient. The evidence for this determination is not yet established.

**Option B — DB-L Carpentry reform, stream, endorsement or revised assessment**

If the residual issue is primarily one of assessment and the absence of a renovation-specific assessment standard, reform within the existing DB-L framework may be the most proportionate intervention. Whether DB-L reform is feasible, desirable or sufficient depends on BPC reform program priorities and on public evidence about current DB-L assessment content (which is currently insufficient — Governing DB-L Finding D). This option remains credible and cannot be excluded.

**Option C — Non-statutory ARC credential**

If the residual issue is primarily one of market signalling — particularly the consumer-visibility gap identified in Part 10 — a non-statutory credential may be sufficient. This option is proportionate if scope and authority are not the primary gap. It does not require new legislation or regulation. Its effectiveness depends on uptake, independent governance and recognisability.

**Option D — Another proportionate government-selected mechanism**

Other mechanisms may be identified through the Engage Victoria consultation process or through BPC reform program development. This review does not prejudge that process.

**Option E — Status quo**

The status quo remains a legitimate outcome if:
- evidence does not advance beyond the current plausible-but-not-proven position;
- existing pathways are found on further investigation to address the residual concerns;
- the market mechanism is assessed as adequate when tested with further evidence; or
- the residual problem is attributable primarily to builder management or system failures rather than carpenter capability.

**Option F — Further evidence**

The evidence gaps identified in Part 12 directly support this option. The current evidence base is insufficient to select between A–E with confidence. Further evidence would materially increase the reliability of any recommendation.

## Part 13 — Final Classification

### 13.1 Classification Selection

After reviewing existing pathways (Part 3), the legal and regulatory distinctions (Part 4), the seven-domain crosswalk (Part 5), market mechanisms (Part 6), alternative causation (Part 7), the attribution test (Part 8), practitioner cases (Part 9) and the residual-problem test (Part 10):

> **Classification: C — DISTINCT_CAPABILITY_QUESTION_REMAINS_PLAUSIBLE**

**Confidence: 45%**

This classification reflects that:
- the residual problem described in Part 10 is plausible but not yet proven;
- existing pathways have not been shown by public evidence to address the seven-domain capability model comprehensively, but this is primarily an evidence gap rather than a confirmed absence;
- market mechanisms are partially sufficient within professional networks but not clearly sufficient for consumer-facing contexts;
- alternative causes account for a significant proportion of renovation failure risk, and the carpenter's primary role in many scenarios is boundary recognition and escalation rather than primary causation;
- the practitioner cases remain placeholders and cannot be treated as verified statewide evidence;
- Governing DB-L Finding D is preserved: current public evidence is insufficient to determine the relationship between DB-L Carpentry assessment and the seven-domain model; and
- the status quo and DB-L reform remain legitimate outcomes.

**Option B (EXISTING_PATHWAYS_MAY_BE_SUFFICIENT_BUT_EVIDENCE_IS_INCOMPLETE)** was seriously considered. The classification of C rather than B reflects that the specific character of the residual question — advanced diagnostic capability, professional judgement under concealed-condition uncertainty, and consumer-visible capability signalling — is not a generic gap but a domain-specific question that the evidence raises as plausible without yet proving.

### 13.2 Strongest Evidence for Existing-System Sufficiency

- DB-L Carpentry is an active statutory registration pathway that includes qualification requirements, experience thresholds, technical referee evidence and assessment reform in progress.
- CPC30220 is a nationally recognised trade qualification that forms the baseline for all these pathways.
- Market mechanisms function adequately for builder-to-subcontractor engagement within existing professional networks.
- The practitioner cases are not yet populated and cannot establish a gap.
- Governing DB-L Finding D means the existing assessment may cover relevant domains in ways not publicly evidenced.

### 13.3 Strongest Evidence for a Residual ARC Question

- No examined pathway has been shown by public evidence to include explicit, independently calibrated assessment of diagnostic capability, professional judgement under uncertainty, and boundary recognition in an existing-building renovation context.
- Market mechanisms are not established as effective for one-off consumers, new-to-market employers or government procurement at the residential renovation level.
- The alternative-cause map confirms that Causes 17 and 18 (diagnostic failure; boundary-recognition failure) are the most directly relevant to the ARC question and are plausible causes of consequence escalation in renovation contexts.
- The absence of renovation-specific assessment standards across multiple examined pathways is a consistent pattern in the public evidence.

### 13.4 Strongest Alternative-Cause Explanation

Builder management failure (Cause 1) is the most plausible single alternative cause of renovation failures that might otherwise be attributed to carpenter capability. Builders hold principal contractual and regulatory responsibility under the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995 and related building legislation. Many renovation failures may arise from inadequate builder supervision, scope management and subcontractor selection rather than from carpenter capability deficiency. If this is correct, the most effective intervention may be builder-level reform rather than carpenter-level credential development.

### 13.5 Single Highest-Value Next Evidence Action

> **Seek access to current DB-L Carpentry assessment content from the Building and Plumbing Commission.**

If the BPC can confirm whether and how the current DB-L Carpentry assessment addresses diagnostic capability, professional judgement and boundary recognition, Governing DB-L Finding D can be resolved or refined. This single action would have the greatest effect on the reliability of any further recommendation and on the classification confidence.
