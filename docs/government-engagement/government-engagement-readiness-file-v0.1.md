## PART 4 — PUBLIC-INTEREST PROBLEM

### 4.1 Problem Statement Summary

The strongest current problem statement is that residential renovation work in existing buildings involves uncertainties and decision requirements that may not be fully captured by current trade qualification and registration structures, and that capable practitioners who specialise in this work currently have limited formal pathways to occupational recognition short of full builder registration.

This problem statement is not a claim of statewide failure. It is a defined question about occupational progression and public-interest outcomes.

### 4.2 Problem Components

| Component | Status |
|---|---|
| Occupational progression gap — advanced renovation-specialist carpenters have limited formal recognition pathways | **KNOWN** — established from occupational structure |
| Advanced renovation capability — existing-building work involves concealed conditions, prior alterations, incomplete documentation and progressive discovery | **KNOWN** — consistent with established renovation practice |
| Diagnostic and sequencing judgment — renovation work requires pre-construction assessment, sequencing decisions and boundary identification that may differ from new-construction carpentry | **PLAUSIBLE** — consistent with practitioner evidence; independent empirical confirmation not yet complete |
| Consumer clarity — the distinction between a trade-qualified carpenter and a carpenter with demonstrated advanced renovation capability may be unclear to consumers | **PLAUSIBLE** — no current systematic consumer survey |
| Current builder-registration responsibility model — existing registered builders retain statutory responsibility for domestic building work; DB-L Carpentry provides a bounded registration pathway | **KNOWN** — established from public BPC and legislative evidence |
| Whether DB-L already adequately captures the function — current public evidence is insufficient to determine this | **NOT_YET_PROVEN** — see Part 7 |

### 4.3 What This Project Does NOT Claim

This project does not claim that the current system is failing to protect consumers at a systemic level. The problem statement presents a defined question about occupational progression and recognition. Government is the appropriate body to determine whether a regulatory response is warranted.

## PART 5 — ARC CAPABILITY MODEL

The capability model currently comprises seven domains. Professional Judgement integrates the other six through the professional decision cycle: **observe → diagnose → predict → plan → execute → verify**.

### 5.1 Domain Summaries

**1. Technical Capability**

*Description:* Competent application of carpentry methods, materials and practices to existing-building renovation work.

*Why it may matter in renovation:* Existing buildings present variable materials, construction methods and conditions that differ from new construction. Technical competence must adapt to discovered rather than designed conditions.

*Evidence status:* Partially supported — trade qualification baseline confirmed; advanced renovation-specific technical assessment evidence in development through pilot architecture.

---

**2. Diagnostic Capability**

*Description:* The ability to assess existing-building conditions, identify risks, detect concealed deterioration and recognise when further investigation is required.

*Why it may matter in renovation:* Renovation work routinely involves concealed conditions, previous alterations and incomplete documentation. Pre-construction and in-works diagnosis is a core function, not an incidental skill.

*Evidence status:* Partially supported — the need for diagnostic capability in renovation is consistent with established practice; whether DB-L Carpentry currently assesses this domain is not clearly evidenced.

---

**3. Structural Understanding**

*Description:* Sufficient understanding of structural principles to identify load paths, temporary-support requirements, boundary conditions and when engineering input is required.

*Why it may matter in renovation:* Renovation frequently involves alteration to existing structure. A capable practitioner must recognise when work approaches or crosses structural boundaries without overstepping into engineering authority.

*Evidence status:* Partially supported — structural boundary awareness is architecturally distinct from engineering authority; current DB-L assessment of this domain at the advanced renovation level is not clearly evidenced.

---

**4. Planning Capability**

*Description:* The ability to sequence renovation work correctly, manage staged access to concealed conditions and anticipate the impact of progressive discovery on scope and program.

*Why it may matter in renovation:* Renovation sequencing errors can create structural risk, cost overrun and contract variation. Advanced planning capability includes scenario preparation for likely discovered conditions.

*Evidence status:* Partially supported — renovation-specific planning sequencing is architecturally distinct from new construction; direct assessment evidence in development.

---

**5. Coordination Capability**

*Description:* The ability to coordinate effectively with engineers, building surveyors, licensed trades and owners when scope, conditions or responsibilities shift during work.

*Why it may matter in renovation:* Renovation work regularly requires referral to other professionals when scope escalates. Coordination capability includes recognising escalation triggers, not simply managing a program.

*Evidence status:* Partially supported — coordination as a capability domain is distinguishable from program management; evidence basis for advanced-level assessment in development.

---

**6. Professional Judgement**

*Description:* The integrating capability — applying the other six domains together under uncertainty, progressive discovery and competing priorities.

*Why it may matter in renovation:* Renovation decisions are rarely sequential and clean. Professional judgement integrates technical, diagnostic, structural, planning, coordination and boundary recognition in real-time and under ambiguity.

*Evidence status:* Partially supported — the integrating function is recognised in assessment architecture; reliable and consistent assessment of professional judgement under scenario conditions is the core design challenge of the pilot.

---

**7. Boundary Recognition**

*Description:* Understanding the limits of the carpenter's lawful authority and competence — including when to stop, refer and document — in relation to builders, engineers, surveyors and licensed trades.

*Why it may matter in renovation:* Boundary failure in renovation can result in unlawful work, structural risk and consumer harm. Boundary recognition is not a passive knowledge check; it requires active escalation under live conditions.

*Evidence status:* Partially supported — boundary recognition as a distinctive capability domain is well-grounded in the current project architecture; scenario-based assessment of boundary decisions is central to the pilot design.

---

### 5.2 Important Note

This is a capability framework. It is not a training curriculum. These domains describe what should be assessed, not what should be taught to a prescribed syllabus.

## PART 6 — ASSESSMENT ARCHITECTURE

### 6.1 Core Architecture

The governed assessment architecture developed for this project operates on the following principles:

| Principle | Detail |
|---|---|
| Scenario-based assessment | Candidates respond to renovation scenarios that present realistic ambiguity, concealed conditions and escalation decisions |
| 0–4 rubric | Each domain is scored 0 to 4; higher scores reflect more sophisticated judgment and documentation |
| Critical-fail rule | A critical failure on a boundary, safety or escalation decision cannot be averaged away by strong performance on other domains |
| Stopping/referring as evidence | Recognising that a scenario requires referral to another professional is treated as a demonstration of advanced competence, not a failure |
| Unknown evidence rule | Evidence not before the assessor at the time of assessment cannot improve an outcome retrospectively |
| Assessor calibration | Assessors are calibrated against benchmark scenarios to reduce variance; calibration is a formal process, not a one-time event |
| Second review | An independent second review process exists for contested assessments |
| Complaints and process review | A formal complaints pathway is available separate from second review |
| Evidence status controls | Evidence is classified by strength before being used in any public, government or pilot context |

### 6.2 Core Assessment Principle

> **Experience ≠ automatic competence.**

Time served and volume of work are evidence of exposure and eligibility to be assessed. They are not substitutes for demonstrated capability in assessment.

## PART 7 — DB-L CARPENTRY CURRENT BASELINE

*Based on: DB-L Carpentry Assessment Architecture Evidence Review v0.1*

### 7.1 What DB-L Carpentry Clearly Provides

Current public BPC evidence confirms that DB-L Carpentry provides:

- **Broad carpentry and renovation scope** — DB-L Carpentry authorises domestic building work limited to carpentry work, covering a practical range of residential renovation activities.
- **Trade qualification baseline** — Certificate III in Carpentry with prescribed units.
- **Practical experience requirements** — at least 3 years practical experience, with Technical Referee verification.
- **Technical Referee Report** — a class-specific evidence artifact requiring verification by a named referee.
- **Registration framework** — inclusion in the registered-builder framework administered by BPC, with its associated obligations.
- **Financial and consumer responsibility framework** — including Minimum Financial Requirements (from 1 July 2026) and Home Warranty arrangements for eligible projects.
- **Contracting authority within scope** — the ability to contract for domestic building work within the DB-L Carpentry class scope.

### 7.2 Current Assessment Architecture Finding

> **Finding D — Current public evidence is insufficient to determine the relationship between the DB-L Carpentry assessment and the ARC seven-domain capability model.**
>
> **Confidence: 75%**

This finding means that the current public BPC material reviewed does not permit a confident positive or negative determination of whether DB-L Carpentry currently assesses the seven ARC capability domains — either individually or collectively.

### 7.3 Critical Control

> **NOT_CLEARLY_EVIDENCED ≠ capability absent.**

This project does not portray DB-L Carpentry as deficient. The absence of publicly visible evidence of assessment against a particular domain does not mean the domain is not assessed in practice. This uncertainty must be preserved in any government response.

## PART 9 — CONTROLLED PILOT

### 9.1 Purpose of the Pilot

The controlled pilot tests whether ARC capability — as defined by the seven-domain model — can be assessed reliably, consistently and transparently enough to inform government consideration of future recognition.

The pilot does NOT grant statutory authority of any kind. Participants remain within their existing lawful authority throughout.

### 9.2 Pilot Architecture

| Stage | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Eligibility screening | Verify trade qualification, experience and consent |
| Evidence preparation | Candidate prepares portfolio of renovation work evidence |
| Scenario-based assessment | Assessor presents renovation scenarios; candidate responds |
| Critical-fail / boundary review | Review of any critical-fail determinations before outcome is finalised |
| Second review | Independent review for contested or marginal outcomes |
| Assessor variance monitoring | Identification and resolution of scoring variance between assessors |
| Consumer comprehension testing | Testing whether the capability classification is understood correctly by consumers |
| Aggregate evaluation | Collection and analysis of outcomes data across the pilot cohort |
| Public-interest evaluation | Assessment of whether pilot evidence is sufficient and favourable for government recommendation |
| Government decision support | Structured evidence and analysis provided to government for its own policy determination |

### 9.3 Possible Pilot Closeout Outcomes

| Outcome | Meaning |
|---|---|
| PROCEED_TO_POLICY_EVALUATION | Evidence is sufficient and reliable enough to support government consideration |
| FURTHER_PILOT_REQUIRED | Evidence base is insufficient; further pilot rounds are recommended |
| FRAMEWORK_REVISION_REQUIRED | Assessment architecture requires revision before further pilots |
| LEGAL_OR_REGULATORY_REVIEW_REQUIRED | Legal questions must be resolved before proceeding |
| INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE | The pilot produced insufficient evidence to support any conclusion |
| NO_FURTHER_ACTION_RECOMMENDED | Evidence does not support further pursuit of formal recognition |

## PART 12 — WHAT GOVERNMENT WOULD NEED TO DECIDE

This section sets out the questions government would need to address before determining any formal response to this project.

1. **Is there a distinct practitioner function?** Is advanced residential renovation carpentry sufficiently distinct from general carpentry — and from domestic building generally — to warrant defined competence assessment and role boundaries?

2. **Is the seven-domain capability model materially different from existing DB-L assessment?** Does the ARC capability model describe capabilities not currently assessed through the DB-L Carpentry assessment pathway? This requires direct engagement with the current assessment architecture, not inference from public material alone.

3. **Is additional recognition necessary?** Even if the function is distinct and the capability model is materially different, is there a sufficient public-interest basis for formal recognition? What consumer, occupational or safety benefit would recognition achieve?

4. **If yes, what recognition mechanism is proportionate?** Which of options A–F (or another mechanism) is proportionate to the demonstrated public-interest need? Proportionality must account for regulatory burden, industry impact and consumer outcomes.

5. **What scope boundaries are required?** If a new recognition category is created or an existing one is reformed, what work is within scope, what is excluded, and how are the boundaries with registered builders, engineers, building surveyors and licensed trades maintained?

6. **What consumer-title language would avoid confusion?** If a new title is created or endorsed, what language prevents consumers from incorrectly inferring registration, builder authority or contracting entitlement beyond what the recognition actually confers?

7. **What assessment reliability standard is required?** What level of assessor consistency, independent validation and evidence quality would government require before any formal recognition decision?

8. **What statutory or regulatory vehicle is available?** Which parts of the existing framework — Building Act, Building Regulations, BPC administrative powers, or other instruments — can accommodate the chosen mechanism without creating unintended consequences?

9. **What insurance and financial responsibility model applies?** If practitioners hold a new recognition category but are not registered builders, what insurance obligations apply? What financial responsibility arrangements protect consumers?

10. **Is a controlled pilot justified before any recognition decision?** Would government benefit from a structured evidence-generation exercise before committing to a regulatory response?

## PART 15 — CONSULTATION RESPONSE METHOD

The following workflow applies from the moment Engage Victoria consultation material is released.

**STEP 1 — Archive**
Capture and archive the exact official consultation material, including URL, access date and version. Do not rely on summaries.

**STEP 2 — Question register**
Create a consultation question register using the template in Part 16. Record every question the consultation asks.

**STEP 3 — Separate government framing**
Separate:
- the problem government has stated
- the options government has put forward
- the evidence government has relied upon
- the specific questions government is asking
- matters explicitly excluded from scope
- implementation constraints government has identified

**STEP 4 — Evidence mapping**
Map each consultation question to existing project evidence. Use the evidence classification in Part 17.

**STEP 5 — Gap identification**
Identify for each question whether the project has:
- a supported answer
- a partial answer
- an unknown
- a need for additional evidence before the consultation deadline

**STEP 6 — Draft in government terminology**
Draft the response using the government's own terminology and framing. Do not impose project-internal terminology on government consultation questions.

**STEP 7 — Red-team review**
Conduct a hostile review of the draft. Identify claims that are overstated, unsupported or inconsistent with the evidence classification.

**STEP 8 — Legal and regulatory wording QC**
Check all statements about legislation, regulation, BPC powers and statutory vehicles against current verified sources.

**STEP 9 — Submit only what answers the consultation**
Do not attach materials that are not responsive to a specific consultation question. Do not use the submission to advocate for positions the consultation has not asked about.

**STEP 10 — Archive**
Archive the final submission, the consultation questions, the evidence mapping and the provenance of each claim.
